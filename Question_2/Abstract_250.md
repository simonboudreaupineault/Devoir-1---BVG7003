**Abstract:**

The number of retracted scientific publications has risen dramatically, but it is unclear whether this reflects more flawed articles being published or better detection of problematic papers. To explore this issue, we analyzed 2,047 retracted articles indexed in PubMed, focusing on the time between publication and retraction (time-to-retraction). The mean time-to-retraction was 32.91 months, but articles published before 2002 took significantly longer to retract (49.82 months) than those published after 2002 (23.82 months), suggesting that journals now retract articles more quickly.

High-impact-factor journals tended to retract articles faster, but impact factor explained only 1% of the variance in time-to-retraction. Greater scrutiny in prominent journals therefore cannot fully explain the rise in retractions.

The reasons for retraction have also expanded over time. Previously rare offenses such as plagiarism and duplicate publication are now more frequent, with plagiarism accounting for 9.4% of all retractions. Together with the progressive decline in time-to-retraction since 2000, this points to lowered barriers to retraction, likely reflecting recent changes in institutional and editorial policies.

Authors with a single retraction have also become more prominent: from 1993 to 2012 they accounted for 63.1% of retracted papers, significantly more than the 46% from 1972 to 1992. Retractions are thus now more broadly distributed across the scientific community rather than concentrated among a few repeat offenders.

In conclusion, the rise in retractions reflects both lower barriers to publishing flawed articles and lower barriers to retracting them, underscoring the need for continued vigilance to protect the integrity of the scientific record.
